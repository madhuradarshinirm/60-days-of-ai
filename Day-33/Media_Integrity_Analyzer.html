Before we begin

Why media integrity matters

Every day you scroll past hundreds of headlines and posts competing for your attention. Some inform you. Others are engineered — with specific words, framing, and emotional triggers — to make you click, share, or feel a certain way before you've thought about it.

This isn't about spotting "fake news." It's about noticing the small techniques — exaggeration, emotional loading, selective framing — that shape how you feel about something before you even reach the facts.

You'll see this today: in a group chat forward, a trending headline, a product review, or a caption designed to make you stop scrolling.

You'll go through two short, hands-on challenges. In each one, you'll observe first, make your own judgment, then reveal the analysis. No prior knowledge needed — just pay attention.

Challenge 1 · Headline Detective

Headlines are written to be clicked, not just read

Publishers know that a headline has about two seconds to earn your click. That pressure often pushes headlines toward exaggeration — turning "a study found a possible link" into "Scientists SHOCKED by discovery."

Notice this next time a headline uses ALL CAPS, a shocking claim, or a vague teaser like "you won't believe what happened next."

Challenge 2 · Emotion Detector

Feelings move faster than facts

Content that makes you feel something strongly — fear, outrage, pride, urgency — spreads faster than neutral content. This isn't an accident. Certain word choices are specifically chosen to trigger a reaction before your critical thinking kicks in.

Think of a post that made you angry or anxious within the first sentence — that reaction was likely the point, not a side effect.
